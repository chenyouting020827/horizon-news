## [Hijacking the PS5's RTMP stream to send gameplay to Discord](https://yashgarg.dev/posts/hijacking-ps5-rtmp-stream/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A blog post by Yash Garg (published at yashgarg.dev) explains how to intercept the RTMP stream that a PlayStation 5 pushes out when it broadcasts gameplay, and redirect that feed to Discord instead of the usual streaming platform. The write-up is a reverse-engineering deep dive that turns the console's built-in streaming pipeline into a general-purpose capture source. The PS5 has no native way to share gameplay to Discord, which is where most of its players actually hang out, so this technique fills a real gap without any extra capture hardware. It also demonstrates how a closed console's proprietary streaming pipeline can be repurposed by users, a pattern that matters to anyone working on console streaming, capture, or media tooling. The trick relies on man-in-the-middle style interception of the console's outbound stream rather than on any official API, which raises a notable inconsistency flagged by commenters: the PS5 reportedly uses RTMPS (RTMP over TLS) when pushing to Twitch, yet the write-up describes working with plain RTMP. Since RTMP itself carries audio, video and metadata largely in the clear, the interception approach inherits those security properties.

hackernews · ibobev · Sep 28, 15:35 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49879702)

**Background**: RTMP (Real-Time Messaging Protocol) is a TCP-based protocol originally developed by Macromedia and later owned by Adobe for delivering audio, video and data between an encoder and a media server; it is still widely used for ingesting live streams into Twitch, YouTube, Facebook and similar platforms because of its low latency. RTMPS is simply RTMP wrapped in TLS for encryption. Game consoles such as the PS5 build an encoder into the system so players can broadcast directly, and community tools like Lightstream have historically inserted themselves into that pipeline to add overlays or extra destinations.

## [Nvidia proposes a dedicated watchdog chip for every AI agent](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/28/nvidia-releases.html) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Nvidia has proposed placing a dedicated watchdog chip alongside every AI agent as a hardware-level mechanism for improving the safety and security of autonomous AI systems. The proposal, reported by CNBC and discussed heavily on Hacker News, would move agent oversight from pure software controls into dedicated silicon. If adopted, the idea would make Nvidia hardware the de facto gatekeeper for agent safety, potentially reshaping how enterprises deploy autonomous agents and how regulators think about enforceable AI guardrails. It also intensifies the debate over regulatory capture, since the company proposing the safety mechanism is the same one that profits from selling the compute AI agents run on. Watchdog timers are traditionally simple timers that reboot a processor when expected 'heartbeat' signals stop arriving, so applying them to AI agents raises hard questions about what counts as a heartbeat for a system that is designed to operate with broad, unattended access. Commenters also noted that any such chip would need to be open and not controlled by a single vendor to be trustworthy.

hackernews · jonbaer · Sep 28, 15:46 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49879883)

**Background**: A watchdog timer (WDT) is an electronic or software timer used to detect and recover from computer malfunctions: during normal operation the system regularly restarts the timer, and if a hardware fault or program error prevents that, the timer expires and triggers corrective action such as placing the system in a safe state or rebooting it. Microcontrollers often include an on-chip watchdog, while other systems use a nearby chip connected to the CPU. AI agents are software systems that autonomously plan and execute multi-step tasks, often with broad access to tools, files, and networks — which is exactly why the idea of a hardware kill-switch or monitor next to them is being floated.
