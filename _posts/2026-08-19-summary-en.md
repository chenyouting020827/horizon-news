It extends Ornith-1.0's self-scaffolding framework into a full self-improvement loop, where the model proposes tasks, generates task-specific scaffolds, and produces solution rollouts for reinforcement learning. This release is significant for open-source AI enthusiasts because the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) variants can run on consumer hardware while delivering performance comparable to much larger models like Qwen and even Claude Opus. Community benchmarks suggest Ornith-1.5 achieves higher speed and efficiency than Qwen3.8 27B, potentially making state-of-the-art agentic coding more accessible. **Background**: Self-scaffolding is an AI technique where the model writes its own task-specific harness or workflow instead of relying on a human-designed prompt template; Ornith-1.0 introduced this idea, and Ornith-1.5 turns it into a continuous self-improvement loop using reinforcement learning. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is an architecture that activates only a subset of model parameters per token, allowing large models to run more efficiently on limited hardware. These two concepts are key to understanding why Ornith-1.5 appeals to local-model users who cannot afford massive GPU clusters.

## [How Kubernetes Probes Work: A Practical Guide](https://ngrok.com/blog/probes) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The article from ngrok provides a clear, practical explanation of Kubernetes readiness, liveness, and startup probes, distinguishing their roles and configuration. It has gained attention for being more comprehensible than official Kubernetes documentation, while also sparking debate on SRE best practices. Probes are central to Kubernetes health management and deployment rollouts; misconfiguring them can cause downtime and availability issues. This guide lowers the barrier to understanding them, benefiting developers and platform engineers, and the community debate it generated highlights unresolved best practices in real-world operations. The article explains that startup probes pause liveness and readiness checks until initialization completes, readiness probes control traffic routing, and liveness probes restart unhealthy containers. It also discusses controversial practices like failing readiness on upstream dependency failures, which the SRE commenter argues can help clear bad DNS caches and stuck TCP connections.

hackernews · cyndunlop · Aug 19, 16:25 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49363665)

**Background**: Kubernetes probes are health checks performed by the kubelet on containers. There are three types: liveness probes determine whether a container is alive and should be restarted; readiness probes determine whether a container is ready to serve traffic; startup probes protect slow-starting containers from being killed by liveness probes before initialization finishes. These probes are essential for maintaining application availability during rolling updates and pod lifecycle management. The official Kubernetes documentation covers these concepts, but the ngrok blog post is praised for explaining them more clearly.
