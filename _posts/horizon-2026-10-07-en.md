## [Chemistry Nobel awarded for solving the mystery of life's asymmetry](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6ly038jg0d4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) ⭐️ 9.0/10

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry has been awarded to a French and a Japanese scientist for explaining the origin of life's asymmetry — the puzzle of why biological molecules exist in only one of two mirror-image forms. The award recognizes work on how a single handedness (homochirality) came to dominate in living systems. Homochirality is a defining feature of all known life and a central question in origins-of-life research, so a Nobel recognition elevates asymmetric chemistry and autocatalysis to the top tier of scientific achievement. It could accelerate interest in chiral catalysis, pharmaceutical synthesis of single-enantiomer drugs, and prebiotic chemistry. Chirality describes objects that cannot be superimposed on their mirror images, like left and right hands; enantiomers have identical properties in achiral environments, yet biology almost exclusively uses left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars. The mechanism behind this bias — how a tiny enantiomeric excess could be amplified to near-100% — is the crux of the mystery the laureates addressed.

rss · BBC World · Oct 7, 11:51

**Background**: Chirality (from the Greek for 'hand') is the property of an object not being identical to its mirror image; human hands are the classic example. In chemistry, chiral molecules exist as two mirror-image forms called enantiomers, labeled L (left) and D (right) by how they rotate polarized light. Ordinary abiotic chemical reactions tend to produce racemic mixtures with equal amounts of both forms, yet life is strikingly homochiral: 19 of the 20 protein-building amino acids are left-handed, and biological sugars are right-handed. How this uniformity arose from a racemic prebiotic world is one of the deepest unsolved questions in chemistry and biology.

Competing next-generation formats include AVIF and Google's older WebP, so the question of which formats browsers support directly shapes what the web can ship.

The claim that an LLM formally verified blow-up of Navier–Stokes solutions was presented as a landmark for AI-assisted mathematics, touching a Clay Millennium Prize problem. If the formalization does not capture the intended theorem, it raises fundamental questions about the reliability of LLM-assisted formalization and about the equivalence between natural-language and machine-checked proofs across the field. ## [Margaret Hamilton, Who Led Apollo Flight Software, Dies at 90](https://news.mit.edu/2026/margaret-hamilton-computing-pioneer-dies-1007) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Margaret Hamilton, the computer scientist who directed the Software Engineering Division at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory and led development of the onboard flight software for NASA's Apollo Guidance Computer, has died at 90, as reported by MIT News. She is widely credited with coining the term "software engineering" to describe a discipline that was then regarded as art and magic rather than engineering. Hamilton's work helped transform software from an ad-hoc craft into a rigorous engineering discipline, and her team's priority-driven design saved the Apollo 11 lunar landing when the guidance computer was overwhelmed by radar data. The practices she pioneered — fault tolerance, priority scheduling, rigorous testing and documentation — remain foundational to modern safety-critical software in aerospace, medicine and finance. The Apollo Guidance Computer was a 16-bit machine (15 data bits plus a parity bit) whose programs were stored in core rope memory, and it communicated with astronauts through the DSKY display and keypad; the famous 1202 and 1201 executive overflow alarms during the Apollo 11 descent were handled by the software's priority-based task scheduler, which shed low-priority jobs so guidance could continue.

hackernews · muglug · Oct 7, 21:16 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49998895)

**Background**: The Apollo Guidance Computer was built by MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory for the Apollo program and was installed on board both the Command Module and the Lunar Module, providing computation and electronic interfaces for guidance, navigation and control. It was the first computer based on silicon integrated circuits, yet its performance was roughly comparable to first-generation home computers of the 1970s. Because of strict size, weight and power constraints, each spacecraft carried only one computer that simply had to work, and software updates for many missions were submitted concurrently by hundreds of people, making version control and integration a serious challenge. Hamilton led the team that wrote this flight software, which is why her coining of "software engineering" carried such weight.

A new class action has been filed against Visa, Mastercard and several major banks, accusing them of imposing anticompetitive merchant credit card transaction fees. The litigation targets the fees merchants pay to accept card payments and the network rules that shape how those fees are set. Swipe fees are a multi-billion-dollar cost borne by virtually every business that accepts cards, and any ruling that curbs network pricing rules could shift revenue away from card issuers and networks toward merchants and consumers. The case also lands amid broader antitrust scrutiny of dominant payment platforms, so its outcome could influence how card networks are regulated. At the center of the dispute is the interchange and network fee structure that merchants cannot easily negotiate or avoid, since accepting one major card brand typically means accepting the others. Merchants have long argued that these fees — often a percentage of each transaction plus a fixed amount — are far higher than the cost of alternative rails such as ACH transfers.

**Background**: When a customer pays by credit card, the merchant does not receive the full amount: a slice of each sale goes to the merchant's bank, the card network (Visa or Mastercard) and the customer's bank as interchange. Visa and Mastercard do not issue cards themselves; they operate the networks and set the fee rules that banks on both sides of the transaction follow. Because merchants are effectively required to accept these cards to stay competitive, critics argue the networks face little pressure to lower fees, which has fueled years of antitrust complaints and reform proposals.
